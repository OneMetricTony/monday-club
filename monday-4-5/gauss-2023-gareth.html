🔁 Gareth Sequences — one problem, one hour

Welcome back, and happy Monday! Today we get to play detective with a single deceptively simple problem from the 2023 Gauss Grade 7 contest: a "Gareth sequence" that starts 10, 8, where every new number is just the positive difference of the two before it. It looks like it could go on forever in a messy way, but hidden inside is a tidy repeating pattern, and once we spot it, summing thirty numbers becomes almost effortless. Over four quick fifteen-minute blocks we'll build the sequence by hand, hunt down its cycle, use that cycle to add cleverly instead of brute-force, and finish by inventing our own Gareth sequences to see what else this idea can do.

📋 The problem — 2023 Gauss · Grade 7 · #22

A Gareth sequence is a sequence in which each number after the second is the non-negative difference of the two previous numbers, i.e. aₙ = |aₙ₋₁ − aₙ₋₂|. For example, beginning 15, 12 gives 15, 12, 3, 9, 6, … .

A Gareth sequence begins 10, 8. What is the sum of the first 30 numbers?

(A) 40   (B) 72   (C) 34   (D) 56   (E) 64

🎯 Today's goals

  • Generate a Gareth sequence accurately from a starting pair by repeatedly taking the absolute (non-negative) difference, e.g. confirming 10, 8 leads to 2, 6, 4, 2, 2, 0, ...
  • Identify when and how the sequence becomes periodic, recognizing the repeating block (2, 2, 0) and explaining why a 0 plus a repeated value forces a cycle
  • Use the cycle to compute the sum of the first 30 terms efficiently: 32 from the first six terms plus eight blocks of (2, 2, 0) worth 4 each, totaling 64 (answer E), instead of adding all thirty by hand
  • Explain in words why these sequences must eventually repeat (differences never grow, so values are bounded and must recur)
  • Generalize by testing new starting pairs, predicting whether the sequence settles to a (k, k, 0) cycle, and connecting the absolute difference to the GCD of the two starting numbers

Block 1 · Build the sequence 0–15 min

Goal: Understand the Gareth rule a_n = |a(n-1) - a(n-2)| and generate the 10, 8 sequence by hand, one term at a time.

A Gareth sequence has a simple rule: after the first two numbers, every new number is the non-negative difference of the two numbers right before it. "Non-negative difference" just means always subtract the smaller number from the bigger one, so your answer is never negative.

In symbols the rule looks like this:

  • a_n = |a(n-1) - a(n-2)|

Don't let the bars scare you. The two lines around the subtraction just say "make it positive." Here's the whole trick in plain words:

  • Look at the last two numbers you wrote down.
  • Subtract the smaller from the larger.
  • Write that answer as the next number.
  • Now slide over and repeat with the new "last two."

A couple of things that trip people up:

  • If the two numbers are equal, the difference is 0 (for example, |2 - 2| = 0).
  • If one number is 0, you still just take the difference (for example, |2 - 0| = 2).
  • The order you write the numbers doesn't matter for the subtraction, because |8 - 10| and |10 - 8| both equal 2.

Our goal in this block is just to get comfortable cranking out terms one at a time for the start 10, 8. We're not adding anything up yet, we're only building the list and making sure each step is right.

Worked example.

Let's build the sequence that starts with 10, 8. We always work with just the last two numbers.

  • Start: 10, 8 (these two are given).
  • 3rd term: last two are 10 and 8. Bigger minus smaller: |10 - 8| = 2. List so far: 10, 8, 2.
  • 4th term: last two are 8 and 2. |8 - 2| = 6. List: 10, 8, 2, 6.
  • 5th term: last two are 2 and 6. Subtract smaller from larger: |6 - 2| = 4. List: 10, 8, 2, 6, 4.
  • 6th term: last two are 6 and 4. |6 - 4| = 2. List: 10, 8, 2, 6, 4, 2.
  • 7th term: last two are 4 and 2. |4 - 2| = 2. List: 10, 8, 2, 6, 4, 2, 2.
  • 8th term: last two are 2 and 2. They're equal, so |2 - 2| = 0. List: 10, 8, 2, 6, 4, 2, 2, 0.
  • 9th term: last two are 2 and 0. |2 - 0| = 2. List: 10, 8, 2, 6, 4, 2, 2, 0, 2.

Now look closely at the tail end: we just got 2, 2, 0, 2. After 2, 0 we get 2 again, and you can see a pattern forming: the block 2, 2, 0 keeps repeating from here on out. Try the next two yourself: last two are 0 and 2, so |2 - 0| = 2; then last two are 2 and 2, so |2 - 2| = 0. Sure enough, 2, 2, 0 again.

So the full start is 10, 8, 2, 6, 4, and then 2, 2, 0 loops forever. Spotting that repeating block is exactly what we'll use later to find the sum without writing all 30 terms.

But a1 + a2 = 11 is odd, and the difference of two numbers keeps a kind of "odd/even fingerprint" that makes the pair of terms refuse to match up that early here. The lesson: building terms one at a time is the surest way to test a claim, and sometimes the honest answer is "it can never happen."

💡 Common mistakes & tutor tips
  • Subtracting in the fixed order (always previous minus the one before) and writing negatives. A student computes 8-10 = -2 instead of 2, or gets stuck thinking they "can't" subtract. Tip: Remind them "non-negative difference" means always put the bigger number first: do |8-10| = 10-8 = 2. The answer is just the gap between the two numbers, and a gap is never negative.
  • Using the wrong two numbers (skipping a term or reusing an old one). When the sequence gets long, students grab terms that aren't the two most recent ones. Tip: Have them physically point at or underline the last two numbers before each subtraction, then slide that "window" one step to the right each time: 10,8 → then 8,2 → then 2,6, and so on.
  • Panicking at the 0 (term 8) or thinking the sequence ends there. Reaching ...2,2,0 some students think it stops, or they don't know what to do after a 0. Tip: Show that 0 is a perfectly normal term — just keep going: |0-2| = 2, then |2-2| = 0, then |2-0| = 2. The pattern keeps cycling 2,2,0 forever, it doesn't die.
  • Not noticing the repeating block, so they re-derive every single term and make arithmetic slips. Grinding out all 30 terms by hand invites errors. Tip: After they reach 2,2,0,2,2,0, ask "what do you notice?" Let them spot the (2,2,0) loop themselves — that discovery is the whole point and sets up the sum shortcut for the next sub-lesson.

Block 2 · Hunt for the Cycle 15–30 min

Goal: Detect where a Gareth sequence starts repeating and find its cycle length (period 3: the block 2,2,0).

Last time we just wrote out terms using the rule a_n = |previous two subtracted|. Now we go detective mode. Big secret: these sequences always settle into a repeating loop. Once you spot the loop, you never have to compute term-by-term again.

How to spot a cycle: a Gareth sequence is fully decided by the pair of numbers you're standing on. If you ever see the same pair show up again, everything after it must repeat in the exact same way. Same pair in, same future out.

  • Watch the pairs, not single numbers. Slide a window of two terms across the sequence.
  • When a pair repeats, the cycle has begun. The distance between the two appearances is the cycle length (also called the period).
  • Why it must lock in: the difference rule can never make a number bigger than the larger of the two before it. So the numbers can only shrink or hold — they can't run away to infinity. With small numbers, there are only so many possible pairs, so a repeat is forced eventually.

The 0 is the trap door. Watch what happens whenever a 0 appears: if you have a pair like (2, 0), the next term is |0 - 2| = 2, giving (0, 2). Then |2 - 0| = 2, giving (2, 2). Then |2 - 2| = 0, giving (2, 0) again. We're right back where we started. That's the loop snapping shut.

Worked example.

Goal: hunt down the cycle in the 10, 8 sequence. Let's list terms and slide a two-term window across them.

Terms: 10, 8, 2, 6, 4, 2, 2, 0, 2, 2, 0, 2, 2, 0, ...

Step 1 — build the terms (quick refresher of the rule):

  • |8 - 10| = 2
  • |2 - 8| = 6
  • |6 - 2| = 4
  • |4 - 6| = 2
  • |2 - 4| = 2
  • |2 - 2| = 0
  • |0 - 2| = 2
  • |2 - 0| = 2
  • |2 - 2| = 0 ... and here we go again.

Step 2 — list the pairs (the sliding window):

  • (10, 8) → (8, 2) → (2, 6) → (6, 4) → (4, 2) → (2, 2) → (2, 0) → (0, 2) → (2, 2) ...

Step 3 — catch the repeat. Look carefully: the pair (2, 2) shows up at the 6th-7th terms, and again at the 9th-10th terms. Same pair! From the moment (2, 2) first appears, the future is locked.

Step 4 — measure the cycle length. Starting from that first (2, 2), the terms go 2, 2, 0, 2, 2, 0, 2, 2, 0, .... The repeating block is 2, 2, 0 — that's 3 terms long, so the cycle has period 3.

Step 5 — name where it starts. The first 5 terms (10, 8, 2, 6, 4) are a one-time "warm-up" that never comes back. From the 6th term onward, it's nothing but copies of 2, 2, 0 forever. That clean split — 5 warm-up terms, then endless (2,2,0) blocks — is exactly what we'll use next block to total up 30 terms fast.

2.3Why does this sequence get STUCK repeating 2, 2, 0 forever and never break out? Explain it in your own words. Hint: think about what the rule a_n = |a_(n-1) - a_(n-2)| does, and check what happens right after you reach the pair 2, 0. Then explain why hitting a pair you've seen before guarantees a cycle.▸ Reveal solution

The key idea: each new term depends ONLY on the last two terms. So if the same PAIR of numbers ever shows up again, everything after it must repeat exactly the same way.

Let's trace it carefully starting from the part where it locks in. Look at the pair (2, 2):

  • From 2, 2: next is |2 - 2| = 0. Now we have ..., 2, 2, 0.
  • From 2, 0: next is |0 - 2| = 2. Now we have ..., 2, 0, 2.
  • From 0, 2: next is |2 - 0| = 2. Now we have ..., 0, 2, 2.
  • From 2, 2: next is |2 - 2| = 0 ... and we are back to a pair (2, 2) we already saw!

Because the rule only looks at the previous two numbers, the moment we land on the pair (2, 2) a second time, the sequence is forced to make the exact same next moves as before: 0, then 2, then 2, then 0, ... forever. It can never escape, because there is no memory of anything older than the last two terms.

So the sequence cycles through the three pairs (2,2) → (2,0) → (0,2) → back to (2,2), which on the number line reads off as the repeating block 2, 2, 0 with period 3.

Answer: Each term depends only on the previous two, so once a pair like (2, 2) repeats, all following terms must repeat too, locking the sequence into the 2, 2, 0 cycle forever.

Challenge: The 21, 13 Gareth Sequence

The rule again: each term after the second is the non-negative (absolute) difference of the two before it. So a_n = |a_(n-1) - a_(n-2)|.

Part (a): Build it and hunt for the cycle

Let's grind out terms one at a time. I'll show the subtraction for each:

  • Start: 21, 13
  • |13 - 21| = 8
  • |8 - 13| = 5
  • |5 - 8| = 3
  • |3 - 5| = 2
  • |2 - 3| = 1
  • |1 - 2| = 1
  • |1 - 1| = 0
  • |0 - 1| = 1
  • |1 - 0| = 1
  • |1 - 1| = 0 ... and we're looping!

So the full run begins:

21, 13, 8, 5, 3, 2, 1, 1, 0, 1, 1, 0, 1, 1, 0, ...

The repeating block is (1, 1, 0), and it first locks in at term 7 (the first "1" of the steady pattern). After that it's (1, 1, 0) over and over forever.

How did we know it was really a cycle and not just luck? A cycle is guaranteed the moment a PAIR of consecutive terms repeats. The pair (1, 1) shows up at terms 7-8, and again at terms 10-11. Once a pair repeats, every term after it is forced to copy what came before, because each term depends only on the two right in front of it. The pattern is trapped.

Part (b): Sum of the first 100 terms

Split the 100 terms into two parts: the "warm-up" before the cycle, and the steady cycle.

Warm-up (terms 1-6): 21 + 13 + 8 + 5 + 3 + 2 = 52

Cycle part (terms 7-100): that's 100 - 6 = 94 terms, all following the (1, 1, 0) block.

Each full block has 3 terms and sums to 1 + 1 + 0 = 2. How many full blocks fit in 94 terms?

94 = 3 x 31 + 1, so there are 31 full blocks plus 1 leftover term.

  • 31 full blocks: 31 x 2 = 62
  • 1 leftover term: the cycle is 1, 1, 0, 1, 1, 0, ... so the term right after 31 complete blocks is the first of a new block, which is 1

Total = 52 + 62 + 1 = 115.

Part (c): The bonus brain-teaser

The number the sequence settles into repeating (alongside 0) is 1. Here's the slick shortcut: that steady value is always the greatest common divisor (GCD) of the two starting numbers!

GCD of 21 and 13: since 13 is prime and doesn't divide 21, their only common factor is 1. So the steady value is 1 - exactly what we found.

Why? Repeatedly taking differences is secretly the same machine as the Euclidean algorithm for finding a GCD (the famous "keep subtracting the smaller from the larger" trick). Every Gareth sequence eventually grinds down to the pattern (g, g, 0) where g = GCD of the two starters. For example, 10 and 8 have GCD 2, which is why the contest sequence locked into (2, 2, 0). And 21, 13 have GCD 1, so it locks into (1, 1, 0). You can predict the steady block instantly without writing a single difference.

Quick recap

  • Watch for a repeating PAIR of terms - that's the signal a cycle has begun.
  • The cycle here is (1, 1, 0), length 3, starting at term 7.
  • Split the sum into warm-up plus whole-number-of-blocks plus leftover.
  • The steady repeated value is the GCD of the two starting numbers - a free prediction.

Answer: (a) The repeating block is (1, 1, 0), first locking in at term 7. (b) The sum of the first 100 terms is 115. (c) The steady repeated value is 1, which equals GCD(21, 13); repeated differencing is the Euclidean algorithm, so any Gareth sequence settles into (g, g, 0) where g is the GCD of the two starting numbers.

💡 Common mistakes & tutor tips
  • Mistake: Declaring the cycle too early. Students see the early 2 (like in 10,8,2,6,4,2,2,0...) and shout "it's repeating!" before a true block locks in. Tutor tip: A real cycle means a PAIR of consecutive terms shows up again. The pair (2,2) reappears and is followed by 0 every time — that's the proof it's a true loop, not just one matching number.
  • Mistake: Getting the period (cycle length) wrong. Some count the repeating block as length 2 (just "2,0") or length 4, instead of the correct 3 (2,2,0). Tutor tip: Write the terms in rows of 3 and stack them: 2,2,0 / 2,2,0 / 2,2,0. When the columns line up perfectly, the row length is your period. Here three terms per row line up, so the period is 3.
  • Mistake: Marking the wrong starting point of the cycle. Students assume the cycle starts at term 1 and try to fit 10,8,2,6,4 into the pattern. Tutor tip: The "settling-in" terms (10,8,2,6,4) come BEFORE the loop. Circle the first 2,2,0 that begins repeating — that's term 6. Keep those first 5 terms separate when you add things up.
  • Mistake: Not understanding WHY it locks into a cycle. Students treat the repeat as luck. Tutor tip: Once a 0 appears, the next term is |2-0|=2, then |0-2|... wait, carefully: after 2,0 you get |0-2|... walk it: 2,2,0 → 2,0 gives 2, then 2,2 gives 0, forever. Since differences can never grow bigger than the numbers you start with, the sequence is trapped in small numbers and MUST eventually repeat a pair — and once any pair repeats, everything after must repeat too.

Block 3 · Sum Using the Cycle 30–45 min

Goal: Find the sum of the first 30 Gareth-sequence terms by adding the pre-cycle part, then counting full cycles plus leftovers.

Now we cash in everything we learned. We don't want to add 30 numbers one by one. Instead we use the cycle to do the heavy lifting. Here's the game plan, in 4 steps:

  • Step 1 - Split the sequence. Write out enough terms to see where the repeating block starts. For 10, 8 the terms are: 10, 8, 2, 6, 4, then 2, 2, 0 repeating forever. So the first 5 terms come before the cycle, and everything after is the block (2, 2, 0) over and over.
  • Step 2 - Add the "before" part. Just add those first few terms by hand: 10+8+2+6+4 = 30.
  • Step 3 - Count cycles and leftovers. We need 30 terms total. We already used 5, so 30 - 5 = 25 terms still to go. Each cycle is 3 terms long. How many full cycles fit in 25? Do 25 divided by 3 = 8 with remainder 1. So that's 8 full cycles and 1 leftover term.
  • Step 4 - Multiply and add. One cycle (2, 2, 0) sums to 4. Eight cycles give 8 x 4 = 32. The 1 leftover term is the next term in the block, which is the first 2. Add it all: 30 + 32 + 2 = 64.

The big idea: total = (before-sum) + (number of full cycles)x(cycle-sum) + (leftover terms). Multiplication replaces a giant addition, and the remainder tells you exactly which leftover terms to tack on from the start of the block.

Worked example.

Question: The Gareth sequence starts 10, 8. What is the sum of the first 30 terms?

Step 1 - Find where the cycle starts. Build terms with a_n = |a_(n-1) - a_(n-2)|:

  • |8-10| = 2, so term 3 is 2
  • |2-8| = 6, so term 4 is 6
  • |6-2| = 4, so term 5 is 4
  • |4-6| = 2, |2-4| = 2, |2-2| = 0 ... and from here it's 2, 2, 0 forever.

So the layout is: 10, 8, 2, 6, 4 (the 5 "before" terms), then the block (2, 2, 0) repeating.

Step 2 - Add the before part. 10 + 8 + 2 + 6 + 4 = 30.

Step 3 - Count cycles and leftovers. Terms remaining = 30 - 5 = 25. Cycle length = 3. 25 / 3 = 8 remainder 1. So 8 full cycles and 1 leftover term.

Challenge: Sum the first 100 terms of 21, 6, ...

Same trick as the contest problem, just bigger numbers and a longer sum. The plan never changes: (1) write out terms until they settle into a repeating block, (2) add up the "warm-up" terms before the loop, (3) count how many full loops fit plus any leftovers, and (4) add it all together.

Step 1 — Write terms until it repeats

Start with 21, 6 and keep taking non-negative (absolute) differences:

  • |6 - 21| = 15, |15 - 6| = 9, |9 - 15| = 6, |6 - 9| = 3, |3 - 6| = 3, |3 - 3| = 0
  • |0 - 3| = 3, |3 - 0| = 3, |3 - 3| = 0  ← the block 3, 3, 0 is back!

So the sequence is:

21, 6, 15, 9, 6, 3, 3, 0, | 3, 3, 0, | 3, 3, 0, | ...

The first 8 terms (21, 6, 15, 9, 6, 3, 3, 0) are the warm-up. After that, the block 3, 3, 0 repeats forever.

Step 2 — Add the warm-up terms

21 + 6 + 15 + 9 + 6 + 3 + 3 + 0 = 63.

Step 3 — Handle the leftover 92 terms

We want 100 terms total. We already used 8, so there are 100 - 8 = 92 terms left, all inside the repeating 3, 3, 0 blocks.

Each block is 3 terms long. How many whole blocks fit in 92?

92 ÷ 3 = 30 remainder 2. So that's 30 full blocks and 2 leftover terms.

  • One block sums to 3 + 3 + 0 = 6, so 30 blocks give 30 × 6 = 180.
  • The 2 leftover terms are the start of the next block: 3, 3, which sum to 6.

Step 4 — Put it together

Total = warm-up + full blocks + leftovers = 63 + 180 + 6 = 249.

Answer: 249.

Watch-out: the tempting mistake is to do 100 ÷ 3 right away. You can't — the first 8 terms are NOT part of the loop. Always peel off the warm-up first, then divide only the terms that are truly inside the repeating block.

Answer: 249

💡 Common mistakes & tutor tips
  • Counting the cycle from the very start (term 1) instead of from where it actually begins. Students multiply the cycle-sum by 10 because they think all 30 terms cycle, forgetting that 10,8 and the lead-in 2,6,4 come BEFORE the (2,2,0) pattern locks in. Tutor tip: Have them draw a clear line in the list after the 5th term: "everything left of this line gets added by hand (10+8+2+6+4=30); only the stuff to the right cycles." Then they sum 5 + the cycling 25 separately.
  • Miscounting how many full cycles fit in the leftover terms. They do 30÷3 or 25÷3 carelessly and either get the block count wrong or forget the remainder entirely. Tutor tip: Walk it slowly: terms 6 through 30 is 30−6+1 = 25 terms. Then 25÷3 = 8 remainder 1, so 8 full (2,2,0) blocks plus 1 leftover term. Stress that "remainder 1" means one extra term still has to be added — it's not zero.
  • Forgetting the leftover term(s) after the last full cycle. They compute 8 blocks × 4 = 32, add 30, get 62, and never add the final extra 2. Tutor tip: Always ask "how many terms have I used up?" 30 (hand) ... wait, 5 hand-terms + 24 cycle-terms = 29, so there's 1 term I haven't touched. That missing term is the leftover 2 — circle it before adding.
  • Getting the sum of one cycle wrong (using 2+2+0 sloppily). Some write 2+2+0 = 5 or skip the 0, throwing off the multiplication. Tutor tip: Have them say the block sum out loud once and write it down clearly: 2+2+0 = 4. The 0 adds nothing but the block is still 3 terms long — keep "value of a block (4)" separate from "length of a block (3 terms)."

Block 4 · Sub-lesson 4: Generalize & Extend 45–60 min

Goal: Predict the long-run behaviour of any Gareth sequence, see why it always settles into a repeating block tied to the gcd, and use the count-the-blocks trick to sum a different starting pair.

We already cracked 10, 8. Now let's become fortune-tellers: given ANY starting pair, can we predict the far future without writing 30 terms?

Big idea 1 — every Gareth sequence shrinks, then loops. Each new term is a difference, so the numbers can never grow bigger than the larger of the two you started with. Numbers that can only go down (or stay) must eventually settle. They always crash into a pattern like x, x, 0 that repeats forever. For 10, 8 that loop was 2, 2, 0.

Big idea 2 — what is that repeating number? It's the greatest common divisor (gcd) of your two starting numbers — the biggest number that divides both. For 10 and 8, the gcd is 2, and sure enough the loop is 2, 2, 0. This is the same flavour as the Euclidean algorithm: keep subtracting the smaller from the larger and you grind down to the gcd. A Gareth sequence is secretly doing exactly that.

  • Start 15, 12 → gcd is 3 → it loops on 3, 3, 0.
  • Start 10, 8 → gcd is 2 → it loops on 2, 2, 0.
  • Start 7, 7 → gcd is 7 → goes 7, 7, 0, 7, 7, 0, ... right away.

Big idea 3 — the summing trick transfers. Any "find the pattern, then add up the first N terms" Gauss problem uses the same 3 moves: (1) list terms until you spot a repeating block, (2) figure out how many whole blocks fit and what's left over, (3) add = (early terms before the loop) + (number of blocks × block sum) + (leftover terms). Once you see this skeleton, you can reuse it on dozens of contest problems.

Worked example.

Harder variation: A Gareth sequence begins 9, 6. Find the sum of the first 30 terms. (New start, same skills.)

Step 1 — predict before computing. gcd(9, 6) = 3, so I expect the loop to be 3, 3, 0. Let's confirm by building terms with a_n = |a_(n-1) - a_(n-2)|:

  • 9, 6 → |6-9| = 3
  • 9, 6, 3 → |3-6| = 3
  • 9, 6, 3, 3 → |3-3| = 0
  • 9, 6, 3, 3, 0 → |0-3| = 3
  • 9, 6, 3, 3, 0, 3 → |3-0| = 3, then |3-3| = 0 ...

So the terms are: 9, 6, 3, 3, 0, then 3, 3, 0 repeating. Predicted loop confirmed.

Step 2 — split into "before the loop" + "the loop." The first 5 terms are 9, 6, 3, 3, 0. Their sum = 9 + 6 + 3 + 3 + 0 = 21.

Step 3 — handle the remaining 25 terms (terms 6 through 30). These are full 3, 3, 0 blocks. Each block is 3 terms and sums to 3 + 3 + 0 = 6.

  • 25 terms ÷ 3 per block = 8 whole blocks (that's 24 terms) with 1 term left over.
  • 8 blocks × 6 = 48.
  • The 1 leftover term is the start of the next block, which is a 3.
  • So terms 6–30 sum to 48 + 3 = 51.

Step 4 — total. 21 + 51 = 72.

Sanity check: notice this is the SAME skeleton we used for 10, 8 (which gave 64). Same 5-terms-then-loop shape, just bigger numbers, so a bigger answer. The method didn't change at all — that's the whole point of "generalize."

Gareth Sequences, the Euclidean Connection, and a Sum

Part (a): Write out 18, 30 until it repeats

Remember the rule: every new term is the non-negative (absolute) difference of the two before it. Let's grind it out, one careful subtraction at a time.

  • Term 1 = 18
  • Term 2 = 30
  • Term 3 = |30 - 18| = 12
  • Term 4 = |12 - 30| = 18
  • Term 5 = |18 - 12| = 6
  • Term 6 = |6 - 18| = 12
  • Term 7 = |12 - 6| = 6
  • Term 8 = |6 - 12| = 6
  • Term 9 = |6 - 6| = 0
  • Term 10 = |0 - 6| = 6
  • Term 11 = |6 - 0| = 6
  • Term 12 = |6 - 6| = 0
  • Term 13 = |0 - 6| = 6
  • Term 14 = |6 - 0| = 6
  • Term 15 = |6 - 6| = 0

Look at terms 7, 8, 9: that's 6, 6, 0. Then terms 10, 11, 12 are 6, 6, 0 again. The block (6, 6, 0) repeats forever, exactly like the (2, 2, 0) block in our 10, 8 problem — just with a 6 instead of a 2. So the sequence settles into repeating the number 6.

Part (b): Sum of the first 30 terms

This is the same trick as the contest problem: split the 30 terms into a "warm-up" part and a "repeating-block" part. The smart move is to choose the split so the repeating block lines up perfectly.

The full list of the first 30 terms:

18, 30, 12, 18, 6, 12,   6, 6, 0,   6, 6, 0,   6, 6, 0,   6, 6, 0,   6, 6, 0,   6, 6, 0,   6, 6, 0,   6, 6, 0

Notice the clean (6, 6, 0) pattern starts right at term 7 and never breaks. So let the warm-up be just the first 6 terms, and let the repeating part be everything from term 7 on.

Warm-up: the first 6 terms are 18, 30, 12, 18, 6, 12.
Their sum = 18 + 30 + 12 + 18 + 6 + 12 = 96.

Repeating part: terms 7 through 30. That's 30 - 6 = 24 terms.
Each full block (6, 6, 0) has 3 terms and sums to 6 + 6 + 0 = 12.
How many full blocks fit in 24 terms? 24 = 3 × 8 exactly, so that's 8 full blocks and no leftovers — it lines up perfectly.

  • 8 full blocks: 8 × 12 = 96

Repeating-part sum = 96.

Total = warm-up + repeating part = 96 + 96 = 192.

Part (c): The gcd / Euclidean-algorithm secret

Here's the beautiful idea. For a sequence starting a, b, the number it eventually settles on repeating is the gcd of a and b — the biggest number that divides both evenly.

Check our examples:

  • 10, 8: gcd is 2 (largest number dividing both 10 and 8). Settled value = 2. ✓
  • 18, 30: gcd is 6 (6 divides 18 and 30). Settled value = 6. ✓

Prediction for 24, 42: I find the gcd without writing the sequence. 24 = 2×2×2×3, and 42 = 2×3×7. The factors they share are one 2 and one 3, so gcd = 2 × 3 = 6. So the sequence starting 24, 42 will eventually repeat the block (6, 6, 0). Predicted settled number: 6.

Why differences can never shrink below the gcd: Let g be the gcd of the starting pair. Then both starting numbers are multiples of g (that's what "divides both" means: 24 and 42 are both multiples of 6). Now here's the key: if two numbers are both multiples of g, their difference is also a multiple of g. For example, 42 and 24 are multiples of 6, and their difference 42 - 24 = 18 is also a multiple of 6. Since every new term is a difference of two earlier multiples of g, every single term in the whole sequence stays a multiple of g.

So the terms can only ever be 0, g, 2g, 3g, ... They can never land on a positive number smaller than g, because there is no multiple of g between 0 and g. The differences keep shrinking (just like in the Euclidean algorithm for finding a gcd — that algorithm is literally "keep subtracting the smaller from the larger"), but the smallest positive value they can possibly reach is g itself. Once you hit g and 0, you're locked into g, g, 0, g, g, 0, ... forever.

That's why these sequences ALWAYS fall into a (g, g, 0) loop: the gcd is a floor the differences can't break through, and subtracting equal numbers eventually produces a 0, which kicks off the loop.

The transfer skill (for other Gauss problems)

Whenever you see a "follow this rule, then sum a long stretch" problem: (1) generate terms until you spot a repeating block, (2) split the sum into the warm-up part plus a whole number of blocks plus leftovers, and (3) use division-with-remainder (like 24 = 3×8) to count blocks and leftovers. The same three-step recipe cracks the contest 10, 8 problem and this harder one — and picking your split so the blocks line up cleanly saves a lot of fuss.

Answer: (a) 18, 30, 12, 18, 6, 12, then the block (6, 6, 0) repeats forever — it settles on 6. (b) The sum of the first 30 terms is 192. (c) For 24, 42 the settled number is gcd(24, 42) = 6, so it loops (6, 6, 0); differences can never drop below the gcd because every term stays a multiple of the gcd, and there is no multiple of g strictly between 0 and g.
